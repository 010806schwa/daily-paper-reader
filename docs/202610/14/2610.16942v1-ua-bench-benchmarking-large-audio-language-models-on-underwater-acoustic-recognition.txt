## Abstract 

Large audio-language models (LALMs) are fast becoming a general interface to sound, yet how far their broad pretraining carries into specialized domains remains largely open. Underwater acoustics is a case in point. Vessel monitoring, maritime safety, and ocean sensing all depend on recognizing underwater signals, but to our knowledge LALMs have not been systematically evaluated on them. Building a benchmark for this setting is not straightforward. Annotated recordings are costly to collect, and a faithful synthetic alternative has to bring together many interacting factors: the structure of detection pulses, communication waveforms, and ship-radiated noise, and the sound-speed profile, geometry, and multipath propagation through which each reaches the receiver. We address this by proposing UA-Bench , to our knowledge the first large-scale benchmark for evaluating LALMs on underwater acoustic signal recognition. It comprises 375,051 audio records that couple parameterized sources with modeled propagation, so that every record can be traced to the conditions that produced it. The records span these three signal families, and each carries a three-level label hierarchy (13 leaf classes), a templated multi-turn dialogue, and source and channel metadata. We evaluate on a balanced 2,600-record subset with a gated coarse-to-fine protocol that locates each error at source routing, fine-grained classification, or output format, and add a paired silent-audio control to test whether the waveform is actually used. Nine open LALMs, evaluated without task-specific adaptation, reach only 2.0%–13.1% leaf accuracy, against a random reference of 7.7%. The waveform helps source routing far more than leaf recognition (for one model, +29.5 versus +2.3 points over silence), and just four models gain reliably at the leaf level, by at most 4.7 points. UA-Bench thus gives a reference point for these models, and its released training partition supports follow-up adaptation work.

## 1 Introduction 

Large audio-language models (LALMs) pair an audio encoder with a language model and are applied to a widening range of audio understanding tasks [1–3]. Benchmarks for these models have centered on speech, music, and environmental sound [4–6]. Signals generated by physical or engineered processes have received less attention. Their categories are typically defined by temporal and spectral structure rather than by semantic content, so recognizing them may depend on cues that everyday sound categories seldom require. How LALMs perform on such signals remains largely unexamined. Underwater acoustic recognition provides a concrete setting, as illustrated in Figure 1. Multipath propagation, which depends on the sound-speed profile and on the source and receiver geometry, further reshapes each signal before it reaches the receiver [12]. Labeled data are also scarce because annotated recordings are costly to acquire [13]. Existing resources address this setting only in part. General benchmarks such as AIR-Bench and MMAU cover a wide range of audio tasks, but they contain no engineered detection pulses or underwater communication classes and provide no controlled channel metadata [4, 5]. Underwater datasets such as ShipsEar, DeepShip, QiandaoEar22, and Oceanship offer valuable field recordings, yet most support supervised, closed-set vessel recognition [7–9, 14]. And the closest marine-domain example, WoW-Bench, relies on marine mammal vocalizations [15]. What is needed is a resource that brings engineered underwater signals, known generation conditions, and a question-answering interface together in one evaluation. Building such a resource and evaluating LALMs with it raise difficulties on two sides. The first lies in the data. A field recording reflects the source and the propagation path together, and the conditions behind it are seldom fully known, so an error is difficult to trace to either. Generating signals under known conditions can supply the missing control, but it involves many interacting factors. The three signal families follow different generative models, from fixed-carrier and swept pulses [10] to digitally modulated waveforms [11] and the tonal and broadband radiation of vessels [16, 17]. The received waveform further depends on the sound-speed profile, the source and receiver geometry, and the multipath arrivals they produce [12]. The second lies in the evaluation. Recognition proceeds from coarse choices, such as whether a signal is transmitted deliberately (active) or radiated by a platform (passive), to fine ones, such as the modulation scheme or the vessel type, and an overall accuracy does not show at which step a model breaks down. A model may also answer from the wording of a question and from class priors, so a correct answer does not by itself show that the recording was used [18]. We introduce UA-Bench , a benchmark for evaluating LALMs on underwater acoustic signals, meant to complement field recordings (Figure 2). It addresses the two sets of difficulties through its data and its evaluation protocol. To make errors attributable, each source is generated from a parameterized model of its class, covering three kinds of detection pulses, five communication modulations, and noise radiated by five vessel types, and is propagated with BELLHOP under sound-speed profiles drawn from ocean climatology and perturbed geometry [19, 20]. The generating parameters are stored with every result, which yields 375,051 audio clips in 13 leaf classes, each traceable to the source and channel conditions that produced it and paired with a templated three-turn dialogue. Clips of the same source are kept in the same partition, so that propagation variants of one source cannot leak across partitions. A balanced subset of 2,600 clips, UA-Bench-Eval, is fixed for evaluation, and the training partition is released to support later adaptation studies. ## 2 Related Work 

Research related to UA-Bench spans general audio-language evaluation, the transfer of pretrained audio models to specialized acoustics, and underwater recognition with measured or simulated data. Table 1 compares UA-Bench with representative work in these areas. Existing audio-language benchmarks rarely include underwater signals, while underwater resources usually evaluate task-specific models rather than LALMs. WoW-Bench is the closest marine-domain LALM benchmark and contains 1,777 validated questions about marine mammal vocalizations [15]. UA-Bench instead covers three underwater signal families and 13 leaf classes in 375,051 records, together with a fixed evaluation suite of 2,600 records. It is the first large-scale, multi-category benchmark for evaluating LALMs on underwater acoustic signal recognition, with controlled source and propagation metadata for analysis under known conditions. 

2.1 Audio Understanding Benchmarks 

General audio benchmarks measure capabilities across spoken content, instruments, sound events, perception, and reasoning. AIR-Bench [4] includes foundational tasks in speech, sound, and music, and it also supports open-ended interaction. MMAU [5] places greater emphasis on expert-level perception and reasoning across speech, environmental audio, and music. AudioBench [21] evaluates speech, voice, and diverse acoustic scenes. Broader evaluation frameworks include AHELM [22], which combines several datasets and metrics, and VoiceBench [23], which focuses on voice assistant capabilities. Recent work has moved beyond conventional sound-event recognition. Dynamic-SUPERB Phase-2 [6] extends instruction-based evaluation to classification, regression, and sequence generation, with some tasks examining acoustic properties such as pitch and rhythm. GAMA [3] studies more complex audio reasoning through the synthetic CompA-R instruction-tuning dataset and the human-annotated CompA-R-test. WoW-Bench [15] further extends fine-grained perception and cognition to marine mammal vocalizations. These studies broaden the scope of audio evaluation, but engineered underwater signals and variation caused by propagation remain largely outside it. 

2.2 General Audio Pretraining in Specialized Acoustics 

Pretrained audio representations are increasingly used in domains that require specialized acoustic evidence. BioLingual applies contrastive audio and language pretraining to more than one million captioned animal recordings, supporting zero-shot species recognition and natural-language retrieval across taxa [24]. General audio models have also been tested on respiratory and heart sounds, where frozen encoders provide features for downstream classifiers. Their performance can approach task-specific systems on clean recordings but declines on noisier tasks [25]. These results demonstrate both the value and the limits of broad audio pretraining. Most studies in specialized domains use a learned probe, fine-tuning, or further domain pretraining, so they do not show whether an instruction-following LALM can recognize the same signals without task-specific adaptation. Source 4structure and acquisition conditions are also rarely varied independently. UA-Bench focuses directly on this transfer setting by keeping the model fixed while varying the source family and propagation conditions in a controlled manner. The values of these variables are retained for diagnostic analysis. 

2.3 Underwater Acoustic Recognition, Data, and Simulation 

Underwater target recognition has traditionally relied on task-specific spectral and modulation features. Work on ShipsEar [7] combines cepstral representations with Gaussian mixture models for vessel classification. DeepShip [8] compares time-domain and frequency-domain representations and evaluates several learning methods, including a separable convolutional autoencoder. QiandaoEar22 [14] uses LOFAR and DEMON spectra to capture narrowband and modulation patterns for individual ship identification. Cross-domain contrastive learning has also been used to address limited annotations and shifts between recording environments [13]. The datasets differ in annotation detail and task coverage. ShipsEar and DeepShip mainly provide vessel-category labels, while QiandaoEar22 supports the identification of individual targets. Oceanship [9] adds vessel coordinates, velocity, and timestamps to its recordings and supports both classification and retrieval between audio and text. These resources preserve the complexity of real underwater environments, but the source and its propagation effects are observed together and cannot be controlled independently. It is therefore difficult to isolate the effect of a specific channel on a known source. Physical simulation provides a complementary approach because it separates source generation from channel trans-formation. Classical ship-noise models describe the broadband, tonal, and modulation components of radiated waveforms [16, 17, 26]. The Acoustics Toolbox provides several propagation solvers, including BELLHOP based on Gaussian beams and KRAKEN based on normal modes [19, 27, 28]. Their inputs specify the sound-speed profile, boundaries, source and receiver depths, and range, which allows researchers to recompute arrivals or acoustic fields under different conditions. These simulators are forward models rather than evaluation resources, and they do not define a source inventory, label space, leakage-safe partition, dialogue task, or diagnostic protocol. UA-Bench connects these elements in a single evaluation framework by combining parameterized source synthesis with BELLHOP propagation and validating each received waveform. It also retains the generating metadata and uses source-grouped data partitions, allowing model behavior to be related to known source and channel variables. 

## 3 UA-Bench 

UA-Bench is built to show where a LALM goes wrong when it listens to an underwater signal. It combines three components. A synthesis pipeline generates each source waveform from known parameters, propagates it through a simulated channel, and pairs the received audio, called a clip, with labels, a templated dialogue, and the source and channel conditions that produced it (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). A source-grouped partition, together with a balanced, fixed subset of the test partition, keeps training, validation, and evaluation data cleanly separated (Section 3.4). A gated coarse-to-fine dialogue assigns each error to a decision stage, and a paired silent-audio control tests whether the waveform contributes to a score (Sections 3.5 and 3.6).

Source generation. All sources are synthesized at 16 kHz. CW pulses use a fixed carrier, and LFM and HFM pulses follow linear and hyperbolic frequency trajectories. Communication waveforms use binary and quaternary frequency-shift keying, binary and quadrature phase-shift keying, and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing [10, 11]. Carrier and center frequencies are drawn from subbands between 300 and 7500 Hz, while pulse width, sweep direction, symbol rate, symbol count, and modulation parameters vary across sources (Appendix A). A ship-radiated noise source combines a shaped continuous spectrum with discrete mechanical tones. The continuum is amplitude modulated at the shaft frequency, the blade-passing frequency, and one to four weaker modulation frequencies. Each source contains three to ten tones, of which one to three are tied to shaft or blade harmonics, and the ranges are class specific [16, 17, 26]. Continuum and tones are mixed before propagation, so both undergo the same channel transformation. 

Propagation. Each realization is simulated with BELLHOP [19]. The sound-speed profile is drawn from 100 annual-mean World Ocean Atlas profiles along a 15 km transect in the northern South China Sea. Source depth, receiver depth, and range are perturbed independently by up to 10%, and the seabed is silt in all simulations. Every source is propagated through up to two such realizations, each with its own deterministic random stream. BELLHOP returns the arrival amplitudes and delays, from which an impulse response is built (Appendix B). Pulse and communication signals are convolved with one impulse response, computed at the center or carrier frequency. Ship sources use responses at 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 Hz, interpolated across frequency. The convolution is cropped to the source duration. 

Validation and normalization. A realization is retained only if it yields at least one usable arrival and a finite, nonzero waveform. A simulation that returns no arrivals is repeated once with a denser beam grid. Each retained waveform is then scaled to a peak amplitude of 0.95. This removes absolute level and keeps relative temporal and spectral structure, so 7clips differ in structure rather than in propagation loss. The metadata record the source parameters, sampled environment, arrival information, delay reference, normalization gain, and duration. Figure 4 compares one source passed through two independent realizations. Multipath reshapes the local time–frequency pattern of the active signals and the spectral envelope of ship noise, while the class-defining structure stays recognizable.

3.4 Dataset Partitions and Evaluation Selection 

The annotation stage produces records that are ready for use in the dialogue task, but multiple records can share one source waveform. We therefore split records by source identity to keep propagation variants of the same source from crossing training, validation, and test partitions. We then select a fixed, class-balanced subset of the test partition, UA-Bench-Eval, so that all models are compared on the same inputs with equal support for each leaf class. 

From sources to records. A source is a waveform before propagation; a record contains one retained clip and its annotations. The source collection contains 240,000 waveforms: 80,000 detection pulses, 80,000 communication waveforms, and 80,000 ship-radiated noise waveforms. The pulse set contains 24,000 CW signals and 28,000 signals for each chirp class. Each communication and vessel class contains 16,000 sources. Two configured propagation attempts per source allow at most 480,000 source–channel realizations. After channel validation and annotation, UA-Bench contains 375,051 records. Each record represents one retained realization, so records that share a source must be grouped before splitting. 3.5 Gated Evaluation Protocol and Classification Metrics 

Each model receives the audio and stored questions from UA-Bench-Eval without task examples in the prompt. The first two turns yield predictions at the three label levels; the third supplies an explanation for separate analysis. 

Gated execution. At Turn 1, the evaluator compares the predicted source category with L1. An invalid-format or incorrect answer ends the dialogue and assigns cascade error to L2 and L3. A correct answer opens the corresponding branch at Turn 2, where one complete-path option determines both L2 and L3. Turn 3 is executed for every example that passes Turn 1, including examples with an invalid-format or incorrect Turn 2 answer. 

Answer decoding. For the two classification turns, the decoder removes surrounding whitespace and accepts only a single ASCII letter that matches a displayed option, treating lowercase and uppercase forms equivalently. Free-form text and expanded class names are not recovered. The raw answer and decoding status are preserved for error analysis. Appendix Figure 12 illustrates a 2FSK dialogue. Correct source routing opens the Active branch, where option D identifies the Communication family and the 2FSK leaf; Turn 3 then requests an acoustic explanation.

3.6 Diagnostic Analyses 

We supplement classification scores with a paired audio control, analysis across generation conditions, and a check of explanation terminology. 

Silent-audio control. Each evaluation record is paired with an equal-duration silent waveform while its questions and labels remain fixed. We compare L3 accuracy and Macro F1 across the two conditions and report original-minus-silent differences. We obtain 95% percentile intervals for the scores and differences by bootstrapping source clusters, keeping channel variants of each source together. Appendix F gives the resampling settings and additional interval checks. 

Metadata-conditioned performance. Within each leaf class, we divide examples into low, middle, and high strata using empirical tertiles of 𝐺 ℎ for active signals or 𝑆 src for ship-radiated noise. The thresholds are fixed across models. For each stratum, we report L3 recall averaged with equal leaf weight within each branch. Appendix G gives the quantile conventions. 

Lexical explanation consistency. Each executed Turn 3 response is matched against a supporting set of terms compatible with the predicted leaf and a comparison set containing the remaining inventory terms. Shared terms support every compatible leaf. The retained matches define four categories: Aligned for supporting terms only, Concept Confusion 

for comparison terms only, Vague for neither, and Term Stacking for both. The contradiction rate is the sum of the confusion and stacking rates. All rates use the number of executed Turn 3 responses for that model as the denominator. Appendix E specifies the inventory, matching rules, and treatment of invalid outputs and empty responses. The rates describe consistency with the predicted class at the level of terminology. 11 4 Experiments 

We evaluate recognition without task examples across detection pulses, communication waveforms, and ship-radiated noise. The analysis begins with performance over the complete label hierarchy. It then examines source families, decision-stage errors, metadata strata, and explanation vocabulary. This order connects the aggregate scores with the predictions from which they are derived.

Models and inference. We evaluate nine released instruction-following checkpoints. The point estimates show that larger gains in L1 routing can coexist with much smaller gains in end-to-end L3 leaf recognition. Together, Table 9 and Figure 9 indicate that the observed contribution of the waveform depends on both the model and the level of the label hierarchy. These conclusions are specific to the evaluated configurations and should not be generalized beyond this paired control. 

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

In this work, we connect hierarchical underwater acoustic recognition with controlled source and propagation metadata. Our benchmark combines thirteen leaf classes, source-grouped partitions, and a fixed evaluation suite within one workflow. We use a gated protocol to measure source routing and leaf classification, and we relate model predictions to signal families, decision stages, generation metadata, and explanation vocabulary. The evaluated models show limited fine-grained recognition on UA-Bench-Eval. Across the nine strict-decoder runs, L3 accuracy ranges from 2.0% to 13.1%, and the highest value is 5.4 percentage points above the gated random reference after rounding. MiDashengLM achieves the highest Macro F1 at 0.083, narrowly ahead of Qwen2.5-Omni at 0.082. The model rankings also change across the hierarchy and source families. Voxtral-Small leads on detection pulses, Gemma-4-E2B-IT leads on communication waveforms, and Qwen2.5-Omni leads on ship-radiated noise. No evaluated model provides a consistent advantage across all three families. Prediction-level results explain part of this variation. Some models fail because their source-routing answer has an invalid format or selects the wrong branch, while others reach the terminal decision but concentrate their predictions on a small number of leaves. Under the strict single-letter decoder, a correct terminal option identifies the full hierarchical path, which makes the stage-wise error partition directly interpretable. Explanation vocabulary supplies another view of response behavior: Gemma-4-12B has the highest conditional lexical alignment rate, but only 717 responses enter this analysis because its Turn 1 failure count is large. The paired silent-audio results further separate model behaviors. Qwen2.5-Omni, Audio-Flamingo-Next, Voxtral-Small, and Gemma-4-E2B show positive L3 differences whose cluster-bootstrap intervals exclude zero, whereas Voxtral-Mini performs better on silence in this diagnostic comparison. The remaining models show small or zero L3 differences. These outcomes provide evidence about model behavior under the present protocol, not a general claim that audio is or is not useful. The benchmark represents a synthetic distribution defined by its source ranges, propagation settings, evaluation selection, questions, and decoder. Field recordings will be needed to measure transfer beyond this setting. Future work can also expand environmental diversity, add independently validated labels, and compare model decisions under controlled changes to the audio. We provide UA-Bench as a common basis for these studies and for tracking progress in hierarchical underwater acoustic recognition. 18 References
